Why nobody attacks

A Magic player watching Duel Masters will notice something odd: players with lethal boards sitting there doing nothing. Not because of a counter-attack. Because in this game, attacking is how you give your opponent free cards.

Four separate Japanese writers arrive at the same conclusion from different directions. What follows is the structure they are all describing.

The rule that causes it

Duel Masters has no life total. Instead you have five face-down shields. A creature connects, a shield breaks, and the broken shield goes into the defender's hand. If it has Shield Trigger, they may cast it immediately, for free, on your turn.

Being able to play a card for zero cost on the opponent's turn produces far more advantage than people expect. Depending on the card, the damage can amount to having handed them an extra turn. Watson, "Why don't you attack when you can?", May 2026 — paraphrased

So an attack does three things at once: it removes a shield, it gives the defender a card, and it may give them a free spell. Two of those three help the person you are attacking.

What this does to combat

In Magic, the reason not to attack is that your creature might die or you might be counter-attacked. The risk lives on your side of the table. Here the risk is that you improve their position by hitting them, which is a different problem and it does not go away by attacking more carefully.

The standard answer is to not attack at all until you can attack completely.

The all-out attack Assemble enough attackers to break all five shields and land the final direct attack in the same turn. If no Shield Trigger stops it, the game ends before any of the cards you handed over can be used. Anything short of that is giving resources to someone who gets to keep them.

The two-stage attack

The full-board solution is not always available, so there is a second pattern — called kizamu, "to chip" — which turns the problem into a two-turn plan.

1 Break shields to make them spend

Attack enough to force out the Shield Triggers, accepting that they will resolve.

2 Clear up and finish

Deal with whatever the triggers produced, plus anything they cast on their own turn, then break the remaining shields and win.

The reasoning behind it is a warning about the alternative:

Shield Trigger output in modern Duel Masters has grown enormously. Breaking shields and then passing the turn leaves your opponent starting their turn holding a great deal of resource — which is a losing position. Laconner, "Duel Masters play fundamentals, chapter 4", September 2025 — paraphrased

The failure mode is the gap. If you chip on turn one of the plan and cannot finish on turn two, you have spent a turn arming your opponent and then given them a turn to use it. Both writers converge on the same fix: cards that do work on both offence and defence, so the gap between the two stages is defended rather than merely survived.

It changes how cards are counted

Here is where the consequences reach somewhere a Magic player would not look. If attacking is discouraged, creatures have fewer opportunities to do anything — so they are worth less than their card count suggests.

A creature that resolves is counted as half a card, because in Duel Masters there is no guarantee it will ever do a card's worth of work. Yuuri, on limited-format card evaluation, Gachi Matome — paraphrased

The worked example is a mana-accelerating creature. The acceleration half of the card has already paid for itself the moment it resolves — the mana is there next turn regardless. But the body left on the battlefield is a different matter: you cannot attack freely with it, your opponent is playing the same resource game so trades are hard to force, and it is likely to be removed before it does anything.

So one card produces one guaranteed effect and one speculative body. Half a card. This is a valuation framework that only makes sense in a game where attacking is expensive.

And it creates an information game

One more consequence, and this one connects directly to how mana works.

Shield Triggers tend to be expensive, so they are rarely cast from hand and frequently end up charged as mana. Strong players read the opponent's mana zone to work out which Triggers are likely sitting in their shields. Shigure, "Learn how to attack", February 2025 — paraphrased

The mana zone is face-up and permanent. Every heavy Shield Trigger you charge is a card your opponent can see — and it tells them what else of that kind might be in your deck, and therefore in your shields.

The bind Standard deckbuilding advice says to charge your heavy Shield Triggers early, because they are the cards you are least likely to hard-cast. Standard play advice says your opponent is reading your mana zone to predict your Triggers. Both are correct. The same author who describes the reading technique also notes that refusing to charge Triggers, and thereby strangling your own development, defeats the purpose.

The design goal is legible once you see it. Removing the life total and replacing it with cards that fight back means the losing player is always being handed material. Games do not end by attrition; they end by someone assembling a turn that beats five shields at once.

Sources

Four Japanese writers, none of them writing for the others, arriving at compatible conclusions. All are linked; the reasoning is theirs and paraphrased rather than translated.

  • Watson, "Why don't you attack when you can?", May 2026 — the framing of attacking as risk-versus-reward, and Shield Triggers as the primary reason.
  • Laconner, "Duel Masters play fundamentals, chapter 4", September 2025 — chipping, the two-stage attack, and the danger of the gap between the stages.
  • Shigure, "Learn how to attack", February 2025 — the all-out attack as the safe pattern, and reading the opponent's mana zone for Triggers.
  • Yuuri, on limited-format card evaluation, Gachi Matome — the half-card valuation for creatures, and why it follows from attacking being constrained.

The rules described here — shields entering the hand, Shield Triggers casting for free — are mechanics, verifiable against the official rules. Everything framed as strategy belongs to the writers above.
