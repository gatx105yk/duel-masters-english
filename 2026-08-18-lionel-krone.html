The card everyone is arguing about

Lionel Krone — 《「獅福」の頂 ライオネル・クローネ》 — has taken over the Japanese conversation around Duel Masters PLAY'S. It holds three of the five Tier S slots between its variants, and the reaction is unusually split: some players have stopped playing because of it, others are climbing to Master rank with it.

This is a summary of what the card does and what is being said, for English speakers following the game from outside the Japanese conversation.

What it actually does

A 10-cost Light/Darkness creature, race Unknown/Zenith, power 14500, with blocker and T·Breaker. Introduced in DMPX-03 PRECIOUS IDOL FESTIVAL on 23 July as a PLAY'S-original card — it has no paper equivalent.

Four abilities matter:

  • On summon, it creates three shields from outside the game. Three random Unknown/Zenith cards are pulled from a pool that does not exist in your deck and placed into your shield zone.
  • Those Zeniths gain Shield Trigger. A static ability grants the trigger keyword to every Zenith that moves from your shield zone to your hand — so the cards it manufactured can be cast for free when broken.
  • On entry and on attack, break one shield — either player's. Yours, to fuel the trigger engine, or theirs.
  • Removal resistance. When it would leave the battle zone, your lowest-cost Oracle is destroyed instead.
The phrase to understand: “from outside the game” ゲーム外から. The three shields are not drawn from your deck; they are generated from a pool of roughly fifteen Unknown/Zenith cards, at random. That pool reportedly includes game-ending cards — and Lionel Krone itself, meaning the effect can chain into another copy. This is the single most contested design decision in the current set.

Why it splits opinion

The complaint is not that the card is strong. It is that the outcome is not determined by either player. A ten-cost bomb that manufactures three random game-enders and hands them Shield Trigger produces games decided by which cards the generator happened to pick.

The defence is roughly the mirror image: the Zenith pool is strong enough that the randomness rarely produces a dud, so in practice it is a reliable engine rather than a coin flip. One player reaching AD Master rank described it as far too random — but Zeniths are strong enough that there is not much of a miss.

What players are saying

Paraphrased from public posts over the past week, grouped by position. Individual posters are not named — the point is the distribution of opinion, not who holds it.

Quitting over it A player writes that a night's sleep has not restored their enthusiasm for the game, and that seeing a Krone deck at all now makes them feel ill. Another says they will stop playing unless something is done about the card.
Winning with it, and saying so A player reaching AD Master reports the deck is far too random, but adds that Zeniths are individually strong enough that a bad roll is rare. Another reached ND Master with a five-civilization Heaven's Gate build that cheats Krone, Ajisai and Emeraluda into play together.
Enjoying the chaos Posts about improbable outcomes circulate steadily — five Krones in a single Legend Battle, a comeback where Krone produced a game-ending Zenith from a losing position. The randomness that frustrates some players is the reason others post clips.
Confused by the rules A recurring question: why does Krone's shield-break lead to a creature entering play even when the opponent controls a card that redirects cost-free summons to the mana zone. The answer is that the ability summons rather than cheats — which several players did not expect, and which a popular video specifically covers.
Noticing it is not an idol In a set marketed entirely around idol characters, the card dominating the format is the one thing in it that is not an idol. One widely shared post asks, of the format's most-played card, who exactly is this person sitting in the metagame while everyone talks about idols?

As of the 6 August tier list, Lionel decks held three of five Tier S positions across three distinct strategies — a one-shot kill, a five-civilization control build, and a slower control variant — with a fourth in Tier B. A single card anchoring three different archetypes across two tiers is usually the signature of a format defined by one engine.

What happens next

PLAY'S adjusts card text directly — unlike the paper game, a digital card can be changed rather than restricted. Nerfs and text corrections have shipped in January, February, March, April and June this year, so the mechanism exists and is used regularly.

Nothing has been announced for Lionel Krone. The set is under a month old, and the Grand Prix on 13 September is New Division — the same format this card dominates. Whether the officials act before or after that event is the open question.
