How mana actually works here

Duel Masters PLAY'S does not resolve mana the way the paper game does, and neither of them does it the way Magic does. Three different systems, three different sets of deckbuilding consequences.

This is the single most common thing new English-speaking players get stuck on — it comes up repeatedly on the subreddit — and every explanation of it is in Japanese.

The three systems

Magic Tap a land, get that land's colour

Each source produces specific mana. A card costing two red needs two red sources. Colour is counted.

Duel Masters, paper Tap a card, get that card's civilization

Same shape as Magic, but any card can be a mana source. You still choose which cards to tap, and a tapped multicolour card cannot be used the turn it arrives.

Duel Masters PLAY'S Spend from a total; every civilization present is available

You do not tap individual cards. The game tracks how much mana you have and which civilizations are somewhere in your mana zone. If a Water card is in there at all, every Water card in your hand is castable.

The consequence is easy to miss and changes everything. With a mana zone of three Darkness cards and one Water card, you can cast two Water creatures in the same turn. In paper, you could not — you only have one Water source to tap.

Colour is a yes/no question, not a quantity. The rule underneath everything below

Multicolour cards behave backwards

In the paper game, a multicolour card enters the mana zone tapped — you cannot use it that turn. PLAY'S handles this differently, and the difference cuts the other way.

PaperPLAY'S
Mana available this turnUnchangedUnchanged
Civilizations available this turnUnchangedBoth, immediately

So charging a Light/Darkness card into a mana zone of Fire and Water gives you access to four civilizations right away, while your usable mana stays at two. The card pays you in colour but not in speed.

This is why decks in PLAY'S run more civilizations than a Magic player would expect. Five-colour decks sit at the top of the format not because fixing is good, but because colour requirements are so much cheaper to satisfy.

The curve does not have to be smooth

Standard advice in every card game is to build a clean descending curve. In PLAY'S, decks heavy on multicolour cards cannot do that, because a turn spent charging a multicolour card is a turn you gain no mana.

Japanese deckbuilding guidance handles this by planning the gap in advance:

3 → 5 → 5 → 7

Rather than 3-4-5-6, you designate a turn as the one where you charge the multicolour card, and accept that your mana does not go up that turn. The curve is built around the hole instead of pretending it is not there.

Which card do you charge?

This is where PLAY'S diverges hardest from intuition, and it is worth stating plainly because the answer is counterintuitive for anyone arriving from a game with lands.

Charge your expensive cards early Standard Japanese advice for newer players: put your heavy cards into the mana zone in the opening turns. The reasoning is specific to this game — the best cards to charge are Shield Triggers, which are frequently expensive to hard-cast but free when broken off a shield. A card you would rarely pay full price for is exactly the card you want as mana.

Compare that to a game where mana comes from dedicated land cards and the question does not arise. Here, every charge is a real card leaving your hand permanently, so the choice is which card you are least likely to want to cast for its printed cost.

Zero civilization is not a colour

Zero-civilization cards count as one mana like anything else, but they carry no civilization — charging one does not unlock anything. A mana zone of four Zero cards can pay for nothing that has a civilization requirement. Worth knowing before you charge one on turn one because it looked spare.

How many civilizations should a deck run?

Published guidance splits it by deck speed, and the logic follows from everything above: colour is cheap to access but charging still costs you tempo, so faster decks can afford less of it.

Deck typeRecommended civilizations
BeatdownTwo to three — needs to act early, cannot afford dead charge turns
ControlThree to four — has time to assemble
Five civilizationsPlayable at the top level, but the charge decisions are hard. Not a beginner's project.

The three collaboration decks currently on sale illustrate the range neatly: the Savvark deck tops out at cost 7 and wants to reach it, while the Jokers deck starts at cost 1 and wants the game over before then. Full lists here.

One more thing that shapes deckbuilding

PLAY'S is more aggressive than the old paper format. Run more Shield Triggers than you think you need. Paraphrased from Japanese deckbuilding guidance

This is a claim about the digital format specifically, not about Duel Masters in general — the argument being that PLAY'S rebalanced cards in ways that favour attacking, so defensive triggers have to carry more weight than the paper equivalent would need.

It also loops back to the charging advice: heavy Shield Triggers are simultaneously the cards you want in the deck and the cards you want in the mana zone. The tension between those two is most of early-game decision-making.

Summary for anyone arriving from Magic

Magic instinctWhat holds here
Count coloured sourcesNo. Presence is all that matters.
Multicolour lands cost you tempoYes — but you get all the colours instantly
Keep the curve smoothNot if you play multicolour. Plan the gap.
Lands are free to playNo. Every charge is a card gone.
Play cheap spells earlyCharge expensive ones early instead.
