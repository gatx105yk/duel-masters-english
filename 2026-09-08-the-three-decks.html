The three decks holding the format together

From 420 ranked games recorded on 5 September, three decks account for 43% of everything played. Below are their full lists, translated — as far as I can tell, for the first time in English.

Build costs are included, because the gap between them is larger than the gap in play rate.

This is not the same picture the tournament data gave. At the end of July, Dragerion took a fifth of a sanctioned field and Raid was fourth at 7.6%. That breakdown is here. Six weeks later they have swapped: Raid is on top and Dragerion has halved.

Worth remembering that these measure different things — one is a bracketed event, the other is the open ladder. Why that matters.

Thirteen cards cost two. Nothing in the deck costs four or six. It is built to put creatures down immediately, hold the opponent off with disruption — Mixel and Tasuriku punish cheating things into play, Oriotis Judge punishes ramping — and then convert to a kill with two seven-cost finishers.

Raid burns shields rather than breaking them, which means the defender gets no cards and no Shield Triggers. That is the whole problem this format revolves around, and this deck sidesteps it.

Zero spells. Every card is a creature, and most of them are dragons with a cost reduction tied to how full your graveyard is. The deck fills its own graveyard early, then puts down something enormous far ahead of schedule.

The cost-13 finisher wipes every creature at 9000 power or less on arrival, and keeps doing it to the next three that appear. Two copies is enough because the deck is built to find it.

Heaven's Gate cheats Light blockers into play for free. The deck stalls behind them, then lands Lionel Krone — the card the community spent August arguing about — which generates three random Zeniths onto its own shields and gives them Shield Trigger.

Twelve cards cost nine or more. Against Raid, which wants the game over well before then, this is the matchup the format is currently organised around.

The most-played deck is also the most expensive one. That is not the usual shape — aggressive decks are normally the budget option — and it is worth knowing before you assume the top of the ladder is where cheap decks go to lose.

A note on the lists

These are one site's builds, not a consensus Every list above is Kamigame's recommended build for that archetype, published as part of a deck guide. Real decks vary — the pages themselves note that cards get swapped depending on what you expect to face. Treat these as a representative sixty percent, not as something anyone submitted to an event.

Build costs are the site's own figures and exclude the super-dimension zone.

Also worth saying: the usage numbers and the decklists come from two different sources, compiled by different people using different methods, three days apart. They agree on which decks matter, which is reassuring, but they are not one dataset.
